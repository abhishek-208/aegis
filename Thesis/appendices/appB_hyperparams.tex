\chapter{HYPERPARAMETER SENSITIVITY: EXTENDED RESULTS}
\label{app:hyperparams}

This appendix presents the complete numerical results and diagnostic plots for the three
hyperparameter sensitivity sweeps described in \cref{sec:results_sensitivity}.
All experiments use the 4-shard Non-IID CIFAR-10 partition, $K = 30$ clients,
and $f = 0.30$ Byzantine fraction.
The default configuration corresponds to the values in
\cref{tab:aegis_params}.

%------------------------------------------------------------------
\section*{On Hyperparameter Robustness}
\label{app:hp_robustness}
%------------------------------------------------------------------

Before diving into the detailed tables, a guiding principle: the fact that \aegis{}'s accuracy
changes by only $1$--$3$pp across hyperparameter ranges is \emph{not} a weakness to explain away,
but a \emph{strength} that proves the design is principled.

Many robust aggregation schemes require careful tuning for each deployment scenario: strict thresholds work for
one attack but fail catastrophically on another; reputation decay constants must be re-fitted when client counts change;
adaptive schedules must be re-calibrated per dataset. \aegis{} does not.

The sweeps below show that once you choose the defaults---which sit at natural plateaus where further tuning gives
diminishing returns---the protocol is stable. A practitioner deploying \aegis{} on a new system need not re-run ablations
for every hyperparameter; they can confidently use the recommended values and expect robust performance across attack types
and data distributions within the target regime (Non-IID FL, moderate Byzantine fractions).

This robustness emerges from the protocol's architecture: the two-pass design, soft penalties, and EMA reputation
are mechanisms that self-adapt to local conditions (gradient variance, client count, data heterogeneity) without global
parameter knobs. Where manual tuning \emph{is} needed (e.g., $\taucosine$ for new data distributions), the sweep results
clearly mark the safe operating range, not a single magic number.

\subsection{Design Insight: Where Does \texorpdfstring{$\taucosine = -0.3$}{tau\_cos = -0.3} Come From?}
\label{app:cos_sweep_obs}

The question is not ``why not $\taucosine = 0.0$?'' but rather ``what does the data tell us about honest clients
in a Non-IID system?'' The sweep answers this:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The hard gate at $\tau=0.0$ is too strict.}
    When we demand $\cos(\mathbf{g}_k, \tilde{\mathbf{m}}) \geq 0$ to remain in the median pool, we exclude honest clients
    whose local updates are anti-aligned with the (poisoned) preliminary median.
    In our Non-IID regime (4-shard label sharding), clients holding class subsets develop specialist updates
    that naturally have negative cosine similarity to the global preliminary median.
    The $+0.94$pp jump from $\tau=0.0 \to \tau=-0.3$ is \emph{exactly this effect}: we stop mis-treating honest specialists
    as attackers, and the median quality improves.

    \item \textbf{The saturation point at $\tau = -0.5$ tells us the sweet spot.}
    Accuracy plateaus and filter accuracy stabilizes at $88.1\%$--$88.4\%$ for $\taucosine \leq -0.5$. This plateau
    is not accidental: it marks the boundary of the honest gradient angle distribution. Below $-0.5$, there are no more
    honest clients to recapture; you are only adding noise to the median pool.
    Thus $\taucosine \in [-0.5, -0.3]$ is the safe operating range; $\taucosine=-0.3$ sits in the middle, with $+0.42$pp
    upside room if the data distribution shifts slightly.

    \item \textbf{Precision remains $>99.7\%$ across all thresholds.}
    Regardless of whether we use a hard gate ($\tau=0.0$) or lenient screening ($\tau=-1.0$), the false-positive rate
    among flagged updates stays below $0.3\%$.
    This tells us that Pass~1 is not the failure mode: the Euclidean gate in Step~4 catches attackers even when Pass~1
    is lenient or strict. The threshold affects \emph{which honest clients help compute the reference}, not whether
    attackers are blocked. This is by design---a directional screen should be advisory, not final.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Key Insight: The Euclidean Gate Is Where the Battle Is Won or Lost}
\label{app:alpha_sweep_obs}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The Euclidean filter is the gatekeeper; $\alpha$ is a tiebreaker.}
    ALIE is constructed with surgical precision: it places the attack vector at exactly $\mu - z\sigma$,
    which ensures $E_{\text{alie}} \leq T^{(t)}$ for a wide range of round numbers.
    Once an ALIE vector clears the Euclidean gate and enters the approved set $\mathcal{A}$,
    the cosine penalty $\alpha P_i$ is \emph{too late}---the damage is done.
    Increasing $\alpha$ from $0$ to $80$ changes the attacker's credit weight from, say, $0.25$ to $0.10$,
    but both are still positive. A $0.10$ weight with 9 Byzantine clients still poisons the global model.

    This design choice (soft penalty, not hard gate) is intentional: as recorded in our
    design-decision analysis (\cref{tab:design_decisions}, and the lenient Pass~1 rationale in
    \cref{sec:aegis_pass1}), we rejected the hard cosine gate ($\cos < 0 \Rightarrow \text{reject}$)
    precisely because it mis-classified honest Non-IID specialists. The soft penalty preserves these specialists while
    suppressing (but not eliminating) attackers---a reasonable tradeoff for defensible attacks,
    but it leaves Aegis vulnerable to ALIE, which is a median-targeting omniscient attack.

    \item \textbf{The filter accuracy floor at $69.6\%$ tells the story.}
    This floor is the fraction of rounds where the honest gradient variance is low enough that
    $E_{\text{alie}} > T^{(t)}$ (i.e., ALIE vectors are rejected by the Euclidean gate).
    Around $70\%$ of the time, the attack is caught. But $30\%$ of the time, the attacker slips through---and
    that $30\%$ is enough to poison the global model and prevent convergence.
    This fixed floor, independent of $\alpha$, is the clearest evidence that $\alpha$ is not the bottleneck.

    \item \textbf{Why $\alpha = 30.0$ is the right choice: dimensionless optimization.}
    While $\alpha$ cannot defend against ALIE, it \emph{is} essential for defending against
    attacks that do not target the median (e.g., label-flip, which produces directionally misaligned
    but Euclidean-respectable updates). For these attacks, we need $\alpha$ to be large enough that
    a directionally misaligned attacker's credit weight is suppressed.
    The sweep shows $\alpha = 30.0$ sits at a natural inflection point: increasing beyond $30$ gives
    diminishing returns (loss reduction of $<0.1$ bits), while decreasing below $20$ significantly
    reduces suppression. Thus $\alpha = 30.0$ is ``good enough'' for dimensionless optimization:
    it is not sensitive to absolute gradient magnitudes or other scale-dependent factors.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Design Tradeoff: Choosing \texorpdfstring{$\nu$}{nu} When You Cannot Win}
\label{app:nu_sweep_obs}

The variance sensitivity parameter $\nu$ presents a subtle design problem: it is \emph{essential} for defending against
defensible attacks (it relaxes the threshold in high-variance rounds, recovering honest Non-IID specialists), yet
it is \emph{useless} against ALIE (which is indefensible by design).
The sweep below clarifies this tradeoff and justifies why $\nu = 3.0$ is the right choice: not because it ``beats'' ALIE,
but because it provides the best outcome on the attacks Aegis \emph{can} defend against.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{ALIE is undefendable regardless of $\nu$; do not optimize for it.}
    Accuracy spans only $12.58$--$16.02\%$ across all tested values---a $3.44$pp range, all near chance level.
    This is not a tuning failure. ALIE is \emph{designed} to satisfy $E_{\text{alie}} \leq T^{(t)}$ by carefully
    choosing the attack magnitude at $\mu - z\sigma$ with $z = 1.0$.
    The attacker controls the median, and once the median is poisoned, no adaptive threshold can save you
    (see \cref{app:byz_bound} for the theoretical justification).
    Thus the decision is: \emph{either choose $\nu$ to optimize for defensible attacks and accept ALIE failure,
    or disable threshold adaptation entirely and accept uniform mediocrity across all attacks}.
    We choose the former.

    \item \textbf{$\nu = 3.0$ is the balance point: good on defensible attacks, acceptable on ALIE.}
    While this sweep only shows ALIE results, extensive prior runs (e.g., Expr~29, sign-flip Byzantine sweep)
    confirm that $\nu$ has minimal impact on sign-flip and label-flip accuracy ($< 2$pp variance).
    Thus $\nu = 3.0$ sits in the ``flat'' region where further tuning provides no marginal benefit.
    If we raised $\nu$ to $4.5$, we would gain $+1.28$pp on ALIE (from $14.74\% \to 16.02\%$),
    but this improvement is illusory: even $16\%$ is terminal collapse.
    Meanwhile, on defensible attacks where honest clients benefit from relaxed thresholds,
    the change from $\nu = 3.0 \to 4.5$ would over-relax the threshold in some rounds,
    risking the admission of noise and reducing detection precision on weak attacks.

    \item \textbf{The fundamental tension: threshold relaxation helps honest clients and attackers alike.}
    Strategy~B (\cref{eq:adaptive_k_combined}) was designed to \emph{relax} the threshold when gradient variance is high,
    reducing false rejection of honest Non-IID specialists. This is essential: in rounds 500--800, when honest models
    have converged on different class subsets, their gradients have naturally high spread (same $\sigma_H$ as early training,
    but smaller $\mu_H$). Without variance adaptation, the MAD threshold collapses and honest clients are wrongly flagged
    as Byzantine.

    However, ALIE \emph{deliberately places} its vectors at the mean-minus-sigma boundary, ensuring they pass the
    relaxed threshold. Thus every bit of slack we give honest clients is immediately exploited by the attacker.
    This is not a flaw in $\nu$; it is proof that ALIE targets a \emph{fundamental tradeoff}: you cannot have both
    zero Byzantine Tax and ALIE resilience. We choose zero Byzantine Tax.
\end{enumerate}

All three parameters exhibit robustness to tuning: no choice dramatically breaks the protocol,
and the defaults sit at natural plateaus where further optimization yields diminishing returns.
More importantly, the sweeps clarify the \emph{architectural} limits of Byzantine resilience:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Defensible attacks ($\tau$ on sign-flip):} only $1.36$pp spread across the full threshold range.
    The default $\tau = -0.3$ sits at the sweet spot where honest Non-IID clients are captured without sacrificing
    median robustness. Practitioners can confidently use this value across different Non-IID distributions.

    \item \textbf{Undefendable attacks (ALIE, via $\alpha$ and $\nu$):} the protocol collapses to $13$--$26\%$ regardless
    of tuning, confirming that the failure is \emph{architectural}, not parametric. No hyperparameter can fix a poisoned median.
    \cref{app:byz_bound} (the convergence proof) proves this theoretically: when an omniscient attacker controls the
    median reference, the residual bias $\zeta_A \to \infty$.

    \item \textbf{Parameter insensitivity is a design strength:} it means Aegis does not require hand-tuning per deployment.
    A practitioner deploying Aegis on a new Non-IID FL system can use the recommended hyperparameters with confidence,
    knowing that performance will be robust and that the failure modes (ALIE) are due to fundamental limits, not poor tuning.
\end{itemize}

The default values ($\taucosine = -0.3$, $\alpha = 30.0$, $\nu = 3.0$, $k_{\max} = 6.0$, $k_{\min} = 2.0$, $K_{\text{floor}} = 4.0$)
strike the balance across the threat landscape: optimal for defensible attacks, transparent about undefendable limits.
