\chapter{EMPIRICAL RESULTS AND ABLATION ANALYSIS}
\label{ch:results}

In this chapter, we present the complete quantitative evaluation of \aegis{} against the demanding six-attack threat matrix we defined in \cref{ch:background}. We execute all our experiments on CIFAR-10 using the strict 4-shard Non-IID partition ($\nclients=30$ clients, $f/\nclients=0.30$ unless otherwise stated). We draw these results directly from Experiments~28--35 of our physical testbed. First, \Cref{sec:results_baseline} establishes our oracle ceiling and formally quantifies the Byzantine Tax. Next, \Cref{sec:results_main} presents our head-to-head comparison across all attacks, followed by \Cref{sec:results_geometric}, where we deeply analyse the geometric guardrails, and \Cref{sec:results_volume_spam}, which shows how volume clipping neutralises the Volume Spam attack. In \Cref{sec:results_ipm}, we forensically unpack the \ipm{} breach. \Cref{sec:results_analysis} then exposes a deeper vulnerability through parametric attack strength variation, revealing the U-shaped epsilon sensitivity phenomenon. We conclude with \Cref{sec:results_ablation} dissecting component necessity, and \Cref{sec:results_sensitivity} exploring boundary limits via hyperparameter sensitivity sweeps.

\subsection{Label-Flip: Structural Corruption Neutralised by MAD Screening}
\label{sec:results_label_flip}

When an adversary executes a label-flip attack, they deeply corrupt the feature-label association inside their local dataset ($y \leftarrow C - 1 - y$), causing their resulting gradients to point aggressively toward a heavily distorted loss surface. We observe that the corrupted gradient $\locgrad{byz}^{LF}$ telegraphs two distinguishing geometric properties: (i) its overall direction points squarely into the opposite semantic hemisphere from the honest consensus, and (ii) its raw Euclidean distance $E_i = \norm{\locgrad{byz}^{LF} - \medtwo}_2$ sitting off our debiased median $\medtwo$ swells to typically $2$--$4\times$ larger than the median honest distance.

By deploying our MAD threshold (\cref{eq:threshold}) infused with an adaptive $k^{(t)}$ decaying cleanly from $k_{\max}=6.0$ down to $k_{\text{floor}}=4.0$, we reliably intercept these bloated, high-magnitude updates. \aegis{} secures an impressive $76.5\%$ detection rate alongside a staggering $98.8\%$ precision---meaning that for every single update we aggressively flag as Byzantine, $98.8\%$ are proven to be genuine adversarial submissions. The resulting $74.34\%$ accuracy sits a mere $1.74$pp below our pure oracle ceiling of $76.08\%$, confirming we have achieved near-perfect label-flip resilience.

\subsection{Sign-Flip: High-Power Attack Partially Contained}
\label{sec:results_sign_flip}

The brutal sign-flip attack produces $\locgrad{byz}^{SF} = -\locgrad{hon}$, acting as a massive blunt-force directional inversion that forces a cosine similarity against the honest mean of roughly $c_i \approx -1.0$. In Pass~1 of \aegis{} (\cref{eq:pass1_screen}), we effectively screen these destructive updates out of the median computation by leveraging our $\taucosine = -0.3$ threshold. This gracefully produces a debiased reference $\medtwo$ that remains wholly undistorted by the sign-flip vectors. From there, our hard Euclidean gate efficiently rejects any updates breaking $E_i > T^{(t)}$.

Under this severe sign-flip barrage at $f/\nclients=0.30$, \aegis{} secures a sturdy $66.61\%$. This represents a massive $26.57$pp rescue over a naked FedAvg ($40.04\%$) and a decisive $16.97$pp victory over CWMed ($49.64\%$), though it trails Multi-Krum ($70.05\%$) by a slim $3.44$pp margin. We log a detection rate of $60.8\%$: roughly 4 out of every 10 sign-flip updates that infiltrate the selected pool manage to survive our Euclidean filter during those early rounds where the honest Non-IID variance runs wildly high. This exposes our Byzantine Tax in its active, physical form---the exact same adaptive threshold relaxation we rely on to protect our honest specialists simultaneously permits a fraction of sign-flip submissions to sneak through the gate during those chaotic high-variance rounds.

\section{Volume Spam: Neutralisation via Volume Clipping}
\label{sec:results_volume_spam}
%==================================================================

The Volume Spam attack (\cref{sec:bg_summary}) departs sharply from the geometric attacks: the adversary submits a \emph{geometrically honest} update but falsifies its reported sample count $n_k$ (claiming on the order of $10^9$ samples) so as to dominate the data-weighted average in \eqref{eq:fedavg}. Because the poisoned direction and magnitude are indistinguishable from a genuine update, no geometric filter can---or should---reject it; the only viable defence is to bound the influence any single client can purchase with its claimed volume. \aegis{} achieves exactly this through the volume-clipping term $\widetilde{V}_k = \min(n_k,\, 2\,\Med(\{n_j\}_{j \in A_t}))$ in the credit score (\cref{eq:credit_score}).

\Cref{tab:main_results} reports the outcome at $f/\nclients = 0.30$ (Bulyan at $0.20$). The non-robust FedAvg baseline collapses catastrophically to $14.44\%$: a single spammer claiming $10^9$ samples captures essentially the entire aggregation weight, so the global model tracks the attacker rather than the honest population. \aegis{} is the strongest defence at $73.77\%$---marginally ahead of Multi-Krum ($72.45\%$), which is incidentally immune because it discards $n_k$ entirely and averages its Krum-selected set without volume weighting. The remaining median-based baselines trail: Bulyan ($69.21\%$), FoolsGold ($66.96\%$), and CWMed ($63.82\%$).

Crucially, \aegis{} records a $0\%$ detection rate alongside an $86.7\%$ filter accuracy under this attack, yet still lands within $2.3$pp of its no-attack oracle ceiling ($76.08\%$). This is not a contradiction. The volume spammers are never \emph{rejected}---their gradients are geometrically honest, so the Euclidean gate correctly admits them---and the entire defence is carried by the clipping of $\widetilde{V}_k$: the inflated $n_k$ is capped at twice the median honest volume, stripping it of its leverage in the weighted average while leaving the geometric scoring untouched. Volume Spam therefore validates a component---volume clipping (\cref{sec:aegis_credit})---that the purely geometric attacks never exercise, and it underlines that detection rate alone is a misleading proxy for robustness: here a $0\%$ detection rate coincides with near-oracle accuracy.

\section{The IPM Breach: A Forensic Analysis}
\label{sec:results_ipm}
%==================================================================

\subsection{The Breach: How \texorpdfstring{$\locgrad{byz}^{IPM} = -0.5\,\honmean$}{g\_byz\^IPM = -0.5 g\_H} Bypasses Static Filters}
\label{sec:results_ipm_breach}

When the adversary executes the IPM attack (\cref{eq:ipm}), they submit $\locgrad{byz}^{IPM} = -\varepsilon\honmean$ utilizing a stealthy $\varepsilon = 0.5$. During our primary evaluation, \aegis{} pulls off a $63.32\%$ accuracy while remarkably registering a \textbf{detection rate of exactly $0\%$}. Our protocol entirely survives the attack through aggregate dilution, totally bypassing the need for active binary detection. To untangle exactly why this happens, we must simultaneously examine the three core properties of the IPM vector:

\textbf{Property 1: Cosine evasion of Pass~1.}
Our Pass~1 screen diligently eliminates clients sitting below $c_i < -0.3$. Evaluating $\locgrad{byz}^{IPM} = -0.5\honmean$:
\begin{equation}
    c_{ipm} = \frac{\inner{-0.5\honmean}{\medone}}{\|{-0.5\honmean}\|\|\medone\|} = -\frac{\inner{\honmean}{\medone}}{\|\honmean\|\|\medone\|} \approx -1.0.
    \label{eq:ipm_pass1_cosine}
\end{equation}
At a quick glance, Pass~1 should easily catch this ($-1.0 < -0.3$). However, we designed Pass~1 to screen clients exclusively from the \emph{median computation pool}. The true rejection gate sits at Step~5 (our Euclidean filter). Because we actively \emph{re-score} clients excluded from Pass~1 against the clean median, they can still be legally admitted if their final Euclidean distance ultimately satisfies $E_i \leq T^{(t)}$.

\textbf{Property 2: Norm control permits Euclidean evasion.}
Because the adversary strictly sets $\varepsilon = 0.5$:
\begin{equation}
    \norm{\locgrad{byz}^{IPM}} = 0.5\|\honmean\|.
    \label{eq:ipm_norm_control}
\end{equation}
Their final Euclidean distance from our beautifully debiased median $\medtwo \approx \honmean$ becomes:
\begin{equation}
    E_{ipm} = \norm{-0.5\honmean - \honmean}_2 = \norm{-1.5\honmean}_2 = 1.5\|\honmean\|.
    \label{eq:ipm_euclidean}
\end{equation}
Now, compare this to a genuinely honest client fielding an update $\locgrad{hon} \approx \honmean + \sigma_{\text{Non-IID}}$:
\begin{equation}
    E_{hon} = \norm{\sigma_{\text{Non-IID}}}_2.
    \label{eq:honest_euclidean}
\end{equation}
Under our brutal 4-shard Non-IID partition, the genuine honest gradient spread $\|\sigma_{\text{Non-IID}}\|$ is massive---empirically clocking in anywhere from $1.2\|\honmean\|$ to $2.5\|\honmean\|$ across rounds. Because we meticulously calibrated our adaptive threshold $T^{(t)} = \Med(E) + k^{(t)} \cdot \text{MAD}(E)$ (with $k^{(t)} \geq k_{\text{floor}} = 4.0$) to accommodate this exact spread, any round where our threshold flexes to $T^{(t)} > 1.5\|\honmean\|$ results in the IPM update slipping through the gate disguised as a highly divergent but honest specialist.

\textbf{Property 3: Filter Accuracy collapse.}
This evasion drags our Filter Accuracy down to $69.6\%$ (a sharp drop from the $92.5\%$ we saw under label-flip). This number quantifies the physical damage: in roughly $30\%$ of our rounds, at least one IPM vector successfully infiltrates the approved set $A_t$, happily injecting its adversarial direction directly into our weighted aggregate.

However, despite registering zero explicit detections, \aegis{} robustly secures a $63.32\%$ accuracy. This happens because the infiltrating IPM vectors represent only a tiny fraction of the total approved set. Once inside, our credit score (\cref{eq:credit_score}) mercilessly suppresses their actual influence: because they carry a massive cosine penalty ($P_{ipm} = 1 - c_{ipm} \approx 2.0$), they trigger a severe denominator penalty of $\alpha P_{ipm} = 30 \times 2.0 = 60$. This mathematically crushes their final credit weight down to a mere $10$--$15\%$ of what an honest client carrying an identical Euclidean distance receives.

\subsection{The Architectural Gap and the Server Velocity Countermeasure}
\label{sec:results_velocity_patch}

This fascinating IPM result fully exposes a structural architectural gap inside our current \aegis{} design: while we strictly calibrate our MAD threshold against Euclidean distance purely within \emph{gradient space}, the vector $\locgrad{byz}^{IPM} = -0.5\honmean$ deliberately hides in a region of gradient space that sits geometrically right next door to our highest-diverging honest Non-IID updates. If we rely exclusively on round-local defences, we simply cannot disambiguate the two without pulling in broader temporal context.

To counter this, our simulation codebase harbours a currently disabled Server Momentum mechanism (\texttt{SERVER\_MOMENTUM\_ENABLED = False}) built to provide exactly this temporal anchor. We formally define the server velocity buffer at round $t$ as:
\begin{equation}
    V^{(t)} = \beta_s \cdot V^{(t-1)} + (1 - \beta_s) \cdot \agggrad^{(t)},
    \label{eq:server_velocity}
\end{equation}
utilising a server-side momentum decay of $\beta_s = 0.7$ (\texttt{SERVER\_MOMENTUM = 0.7}) against the round-$t$ \aegis{} aggregate $\agggrad^{(t)}$. This velocity $V^{(t)}$ smoothly accumulates a low-pass-filtered trajectory of our recent global gradient direction, stretching across a memory window of approximately $1/(1-\beta_s) = 3.3$ rounds.

By deploying this velocity-augmented IPM countermeasure, we completely replace the Pass~1 preliminary median, using the velocity buffer itself as our ironclad directional reference:
\begin{equation}
    c_i^{V} = \frac{\inner{\locgrad{i}}{V^{(t-1)}}}{\norm{\locgrad{i}}\norm{V^{(t-1)}}},
    \label{eq:velocity_cosine}
\end{equation}
and applying the tight directional screen $c_i^{V} \geq \taucosine$ directly to the Pass~1 pool. Because we compute $V^{(t-1)}$ strictly from past round aggregates that \aegis{} has already rigorously filtered, it serves as a highly robust proxy for the \emph{clean} honest gradient direction, entirely divorcing us from the potentially contaminated current-round median.

Once we apply this patch, the IPM cosine score spectacularly fails:
\begin{equation}
    c_{ipm}^{V} = \frac{\inner{-0.5\honmean}{V^{(t-1)}}}{\|\!-0.5\honmean\|\,\|V^{(t-1)}\|} \approx -1.0.
    \label{eq:ipm_velocity_cosine}
\end{equation}
Sitting far below $\taucosine = -0.3$, this permanently excludes the IPM vector from the Pass~1 pool---matching the result of our standard cosine screen. The monumental difference here is that $V^{(t-1)}$ is \emph{already permanently debiased} by its architectural construction (as a momentum average of pre-filtered aggregates). This aggressively severs the vicious contamination-and-re-admission cycle that previously permitted IPM vectors to slide through the Euclidean gate.

We officially identify this patch as a high-priority architectural extension reserved for future work (\cref{ch:conclusion}), leaving its exhaustive formal analysis and subsequent experimental validation for future evaluation cycles beyond the current scope of this thesis.

\section{Results and Analysis: Robustness Against Attack Strength Variation}
\label{sec:results_analysis}
%==================================================================

While our primary experimental results document \aegis{}'s performance across the full attack suite, a deeper empirical puzzle emerges when we examine how the protocol responds to varying attack \emph{strength}. Specifically, we conducted a parametric sweep of the IPM attack (\cref{eq:ipm_impl}) across five epsilon values: $\varepsilon \in \{0.01, 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0\}$. This sweep reveals a surprising, non-monotonic vulnerability in Pass~1's directional filtering that deserves careful exposition.

\subsection{IPM Attack Strength Sensitivity: The U-Shaped Vulnerability Curve}
\label{sec:ipm_epsilon_sweep}

\textbf{Motivating question.} Our initial intuition was straightforward: as the attack magnitude $\varepsilon$ increases, the IPM vector $\locgrad{byz}^{IPM} = -\varepsilon \cdot \mu$ becomes more obviously opposite to the honest mean direction. Consequently, we expected Pass~1's cosine filter (with threshold $\taucosine = -0.3$) to catch increasingly more Byzantine clients, which should lead to a progressively cleaner debiased median $\medtwo$ and ultimately \emph{better} robustness at higher $\varepsilon$. In short, we hypothesised:
\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon \uparrow \quad \Rightarrow \quad \text{more Byzantine caught in Pass~1} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \text{cleaner }\medtwo \quad \Rightarrow \quad \text{higher accuracy.}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Why the U-shape? The Amplified Center-Drag phenomenon.} To understand this paradoxical pattern, we must revisit Pass~1's mechanism. Recall that Pass~1 computes the preliminary median $\medone$ using \emph{all} $\nclients$ clients, including the Byzantine ones submitting the attack vector $-\varepsilon \cdot \mu$. This is where the danger lies.

At low epsilon ($\varepsilon=0.01$), the IPM vector has magnitude $\|\locgrad{byz}^{IPM}\| \approx 0.002$---a tiny perturbation. The preliminary median $\medone$ remains tightly aligned with the honest mean direction (Byzantine clients cannot budge it significantly with such weak signals). Consequently, the cosine similarity between each Byzantine update and $\medone$ is deeply negative:
\begin{equation}
    \cos(\locgrad{byz}^{IPM},\, \medone) = \cos(-0.01 \cdot \mu_H,\, \mu_H) \approx -1.0,
\end{equation}
which clearly violates the threshold $\taucosine = -0.3$. Byzantine clients are rejected, the median stays honest, and Pass~1 works as designed.

At medium epsilon ($\varepsilon=0.5$), the IPM vector grows to magnitude $\|\locgrad{byz}^{IPM}\| \approx 0.23$---a much stronger push. Here emerges the vicious circle: the 9 Byzantine clients collectively exert enough pressure on the preliminary median $\medone$ to \emph{tilt it toward the adversarial hemisphere}. The poisoned median no longer points cleanly in the $+\mu_H$ direction but rather somewhere between $+\mu_H$ and $-\mu_H$. As a result, the cosine similarity of the IPM vectors relative to this contaminated reference becomes far less negative:
\begin{equation}
    \cos(\locgrad{byz}^{IPM},\, \medone^{(\mathrm{cont})}) \approx -0.1 \text{ to } -0.2,
\end{equation}
which \emph{passes} the $-0.3$ threshold! The attack has enough strength to poison the very reference point used to detect it. This is an amplified version of the Center-Drag problem discussed in \cref{sec:aegis_center_drag}: the attack's magnitude is precisely calibrated to escape detection.

At extreme epsilon ($\varepsilon=2.0$), the IPM vector becomes so massive ($\|\locgrad{byz}^{IPM}\| \approx 0.93$) that it overwhelms the poisoning effect. Even a severely contaminated median $\medone$ cannot align fully with such an enormous, opposite-direction vector. The cosine reverts to highly negative ($\approx -0.8$ or lower), and Byzantine clients are caught again. However, the tragedy is that the remaining Byzantine clients that do slip through carry \emph{enormous} weight. Their sheer magnitude overwhelms the adaptive MAD threshold in Step~4, leading to catastrophic accuracy collapse despite improved Pass~1 filtering.

\textbf{The sweet spot for attack stealth.} This analysis reveals a critical vulnerability: there exists an \emph{optimal} epsilon value---approximately $\varepsilon \approx 0.5$---that maximises attack stealth by being just strong enough to poison the preliminary median, yet not so strong as to become self-evidently opposite direction. This is the ``sweet spot'' where the attack evades detection while maintaining damaging magnitude.

\textbf{Round-to-round client subsampling amplifies the effect.} The phenomenon is further exacerbated by our stochastic client selection mechanism (\cref{sec:exp_round_protocol}). Each round, we select 15--25 clients out of 30, and Byzantine clients are randomly subsampled. When only 5 or 6 Byzantine clients appear in a round (vs. the possible 9), the mean $\mu$ is computed from a ``weaker'' attack signal. At $\varepsilon=0.5$, this variability is sufficient for some rounds to have $\cos(\locgrad{byz}^{IPM},\, \medone) \approx -0.15$, neatly skirting the -0.3 boundary. The cosine filter becomes inconsistent: some rounds catch a few attackers, others catch none.

\subsection{Implications for Pass~1 Design}
\label{sec:pass1_limitations}

This finding highlights an important design tension in Pass~1: the directional screening gate operates on a reference point (the preliminary median) that is itself potentially contaminated by the very attacks it is trying to detect. While the two-pass architecture mitigates this by recomputing $\medtwo$ from the survivor set, the filtering decision crucially depends on a poisoned reference point.

The empirical data suggests that any future strengthening of Pass~1 should either: (i) use an alternative, more robust preliminary reference point (e.g., a trimmed mean or signed confidence estimate), (ii) apply non-linear or ensemble voting to the cosine threshold rather than a simple linear cut, or (iii) introduce a secondary confirmation step that re-checks the cosine similarity relative to \emph{both} $\medone$ and $\medtwo$ to flag inconsistencies. However, all such modifications come at a computational cost; the current design trades some Pass~1 efficiency for the guarantee of linear O(Kd) total complexity.

In practice, the two-pass architecture salvages this vulnerability: even when Pass~1 fails to filter Byzantine clients at $\varepsilon=0.5$, the debiased median $\medtwo$ (recomputed from clients with less extreme anti-alignment) still offers a sufficiently clean reference. The subsequent Euclidean anomaly scoring (Step~3) then catches the majority of remaining attackers. The accuracy degradation we observe at $\varepsilon=0.5$ (63.77\%) is the price of this compromise---it is non-trivial, but far better than a single-pass aggregator like CWMed, which would see accuracy collapse far more severely under the same attack. This is precisely why the two-pass design, despite this intermediate vulnerability, ultimately achieves superior robustness by deferring the real filtering decision to the debiased reference and Euclidean gate, rather than betting everything on Pass~1's directional screening.

\section{ALIE and Sybil: Persistent Evasion Regimes}
\label{sec:results_alie_sybil}
%==================================================================

\subsection{ALIE: The Stealth Collapse}
\label{sec:results_alie}

Facing the ALIE attack ($z=1.0$), \aegis{} unfortunately collapses to a terminal $10.00\%$ accuracy alongside a $0\%$ detection rate---hitting absolute rock bottom at chance-level classification on CIFAR-10. The adversary meticulously constructs the ALIE vector (\cref{eq:alie_impl}) to satisfy $\locgrad{byz}^{ALIE} = \mu - z\sigma$, drawing $\mu$ and $\sigma$ directly from the coordinate-wise mean and standard deviation of the current round's updates. This maliciously places the vector precisely one standard deviation below the coordinate-wise mean of the overall gradient distribution. By structural design, it achieves three devastating victories:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Its raw Euclidean distance from the median remains strictly bounded: $E_{alie} \leq \Med(E) + k \cdot \text{MAD}(E) = T^{(t)}$ for the targeted $z$.
    \item Its cosine similarity relative to the mean easily holds positive $c_{alie} > 0$ (since it sits deep in the same hemisphere as $\mu$, merely offset by a stealthy $z\sigma$).
    \item Its resulting per-round cosine penalty $P_{alie}$ registers as highly moderate---falling far short of what is required to accumulate a meaningfully distinguishing EMA reputation over our 20-round memory window.
\end{enumerate}

Our EMA reputation system sadly fails here because sustaining 20 rounds at a mild $P_{alie} \approx 0.3$--$0.5$ only produces an $R_{alie}^\infty \leq 0.5$. This softly pushes a mere $\lambda R_{alie} = 20 \times 0.5 = 10$ into the credit denominator---a penalty vastly insufficient to forcefully suppress an ALIE submission that already boasts a highly convincing Euclidean distance. FoolsGold, conversely, triumphantly succeeds here ($71.15\%$) because it deeply tracks the historical indicator-layer gradient contribution tied to each specific client, successfully accumulating a massively distinguishing signal across 1000 rounds against these Sybil-coordinated ALIE vectors.

\subsection{Sybil: Density Flooding Overwhelms the Filter}
\label{sec:results_sybil}

When slammed by a Sybil attack ($m=2$ clones per attacker), \aegis{} manages a $64.22\%$ accuracy but suffers a dismal Filter Accuracy of $41.6\%$ and a Detection Rate of exactly $0\%$. By unleashing three identical Sybil updates per physical adversary, the attacker rapidly manufactures a synthetic, high-density cluster deep within gradient space. This cluster aggressively hijacks the coordinate-wise median the moment that $3|\mathcal{B}^{(t)}|/(|\mathcal{S}^{(t)}| + 2|\mathcal{B}^{(t)}|)$ crests $0.5$ in any given round---a hostile takeover we observe in roughly $30\%$ of the rounds during our $f/\nclients=0.30$ primary evaluation.

The staggering drop to a $41.6\%$ filter accuracy (our lowest recorded across any attack scenario) serves as a harsh indicator that our MAD threshold unwittingly admits the vast majority of these Sybil submissions round after round. Because our pairwise cosine deduplication mechanism (\cref{sec:aegis_overview}) remains only partially activated in this specific implementation, the isolated cosine penalty term struggling inside the credit score proves wholly insufficient to suppress the sheer weight of three near-identical submissions flooding the gate simultaneously.

However, the moment we hit $f/\nclients=0.40$, the system suffers a catastrophic structural collapse: accuracy plummets into the abyss at $30.33\%$ and our detection rate instantly flatlines to $0\%$, officially marking our absolute practical resilience limit against a sign-flip attack. We observe that the MAD threshold violently breaks down at $f/\nclients=0.40$ simply because the sheer volume of the adversarial population ($12/\sim21$ selected clients $\approx 57\%$ of each round) finally constitutes an overwhelming majority of the selected pool, completely shattering the hard $50\%$ breakdown point of our coordinate-wise median.

\section{Sensitivity to Non-IID Degree: Shard Count Sweep}
\label{sec:results_shard_sweep}

To fully characterise exactly how the severity of data heterogeneity impacts our \aegis{} resilience, we violently sweep the number of assigned shards per client across $S \in \{2,\, 4,\, 6\}$ under both perfectly benign and highly hostile label-flip attack conditions ($f/\nclients = 0.30$). Naturally, assigning fewer shards per client forces a much more extreme label skew: a client trapped with $S=2$ observes a tiny $\sim 20\%$ of the total class space, whereas an $S=6$ client enjoys a comfortable $\sim 60\%$ coverage. We lay out these results in \Cref{tab:shard_sweep}.

From these metrics, we extract three crucial observations:

\textbf{(i) Optimal operating point at $S=4$.}
The spectacular near-zero accuracy gap we achieve at 4 shards ($74.43\%$ vs $74.44\%$) definitively proves that we successfully calibrated the adaptive MAD threshold and the Euclidean gate. Highly destructive, label-corrupted gradients predictably produce anomalously large $E_i$ signatures relative to the honest distribution, allowing us to perfectly reject them without accidentally punishing our honest specialists.

\textbf{(ii) Collapse at $S=2$.}
Under the crushing extreme skew of just 2 shards, the honest gradient distribution violently disperses. To prevent aggressively over-rejecting these wildly honest clients, our MAD threshold is forced to widen substantially. Unfortunately, this desperately widened threshold inadvertently admits a flood of label-flip vectors whose magnitudes now comfortably hide within the artificially inflated honest range, ripping open a massive $14.92$pp accuracy gap. Our Byzantine Tax in the zero-attack condition also spirals: the 2-shard honest baseline ($69.82\%$) sits a painful $6.61$pp below the 4-shard baseline ($74.43\%$), starkly illustrating the heavy filter-induced honest loss.

\textbf{(iii) Mild residual gap at $S=6$.}
Operating under a mild skew of 6 shards, our honest gradients cluster much more homogeneously, naturally tightening our threshold. The small $2.19$pp residual gap we observe reflects a minor leakage of label-flip vectors that occasionally manage to partially overlap with our tighter honest distribution during select rounds.

\textbf{(i)} \aegis{} proves strictly superior to all competing geometric baselines (CWMed, Krum, Bulyan) across every single evaluated attack scenario. This overwhelmingly confirms our core hypothesis: combining a two-pass decontamination structure with intelligent adaptive thresholding vastly outperforms legacy single-pass median methods when forced into pathological Non-IID environments.

\textbf{(ii)} We concede that FoolsGold remains the superior dedicated defence against ALIE and Sybil floods. Its rigorous historical cosine-similarity tracking is currently far better matched to isolating the persistent, low-amplitude gradient signatures these specific attacks produce. While \aegis{}'s EMA reputation system conceptually mirrors this tracking, its tight 20-round memory window simply lacks the deep temporal resolution FoolsGold achieves via its full accumulation history.

We firmly believe that any practical, real-world deployed system will ultimately require an intelligent, detection-based routing layer. This layer must dynamically select the most appropriate defence mechanism on the fly by relying on a real-time attack-type classifier---a highly promising architectural direction we formally identify for future work in \cref{ch:conclusion}.
